<p>
	<span class="gray-90">😎</span> <span class="pointnineem">Note:<br> I’ve started exploring the tools mentioned in the blog post. The free tools (Options 1 &amp; 2) require manually browsing the site to be archived, and they both do the job. Option 3, which is more automated, involves a free, 7-day trial that turns into a monthly subscription at a price far too high for my blood, so I won't be exploring that option (I’m not fond of the subscription model for software, especially at such a high monthly cost). See "The Tools" section of this post below for more details.</span>
</p>
<blockquote>
	<p>
		All of the tools I’ll be showing off in this post use standard web archiving formats like <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WARC_(file_format)">WARC</a>. You don’t have to worry about any of the technical details, but I’m telling you that because it means there are a lot of really great tools to do whatever you want with them... 
	</p>
</blockquote>
<p class="pointnineem">
	Footnotes from article:
</p>
<blockquote>
<p>
	<span class="bold">WARC</span>: Most of the tools I’m mentioning also use the <a href="https://specs.webrecorder.net/wacz/1.1.1/">WACZ</a> format, which packages up WARC files along with extra metadata.<br>
	<span class="bold">technical details</span>: For those who want a little extra technical detail, though: one of the things that makes WARC special is that it records complete information about requests and responses, including all headers. This gives WARC a much more complete view of the whole process of visiting a website, and makes it possible to archive even complicated sites with client-side JavaScript that make a ton of requests.
</p>
</blockquote>
